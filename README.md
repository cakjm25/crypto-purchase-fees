# buy crypto: What Card, Bank Transfer and P2P Routes Really Cost, and How Gate's Fee Tiers Change the Math

"Buy crypto" looks like one action. It's usually six.

You can put a card down, wire money from a bank, trade with a stranger through escrow, swap one coin for another you already hold, or move coins in from a wallet. Same coin, same minute, wildly different cost. On a $1,000 purchase, the difference between the cheapest route and the most expensive one can be $30 to $85, which is real money before you've even taken a position.

Gate (the exchange behind gate.com, formerly Gate.io) publishes its own numbers for these routes, and that's the part worth reading carefully. Here's what each path actually costs, where the fee tiers kick in after you own the asset, and which parts of the marketing you should ignore.

## The route decides the price, not the coin

Gate's own purchase pages put card payments at roughly **1% to 5%**, bank transfers at "low or zero" depending on your bank, and C2C/P2P at **zero platform fee for buyers**. One of Gate's regional guides gets more specific on cards: about 0.08% in the European Economic Area, around 2.8% in most other markets, and roughly 3.5% in the US and UK.

That spread is the entire story. A card is a convenience product with a payment processor taking a cut. A bank rail moves money in bulk and charges accordingly. P2P cuts out the processor and replaces it with a counterparty you have to trust through escrow.

For context, card funding is expensive everywhere, not just here. A CEX.IO comparison of major exchanges published in 2026 lists Kraken card purchases at $0.25 plus 3.75%, Gemini at 3.49%, and Binance.US at 3.3%. Nobody has solved the card problem.

## Setting up an account you can actually fund

Three steps matter, and the third is where most first purchases fail.

**Registration.** Email or phone number. Straightforward, and the referral code on the link you arrived through gets applied at this stage, which is also what unlocks the new-user task rewards described further down.

**KYC.** Gate's guides describe uploading a government ID, taking a selfie, sometimes a proof of address, and note the check is usually validated within 24 hours. Your legal name has to match the name on your card or bank account exactly. A mismatch is one of the most common reasons a first purchase gets rejected outright.

**Funding.** If you're using a card, Visa or Mastercard with 3D Secure enabled is what Gate supports, and the purchase goes through third-party payment partners rather than Gate itself. Which is precisely why the fee varies by region: you're paying your local processor's rate, not a flat platform number.

Two limits worth knowing before you plan around card purchases. One of Gate's own guides lists card transactions at a $25 minimum, $5,000 per transaction and $10,000 daily, though these figures vary by region and verification level, so treat them as indicative and check the order screen. And crypto bought with a newly added card sits under a **72-hour withdrawal lock**. You can trade it immediately on the platform, but you can't move it off until the window closes. That's an anti-fraud and chargeback measure, and it's the reason people sometimes report "my crypto is stuck" within an hour of buying.

## What it costs to trade and move, after the purchase

Buying is only the entry fee. Trading and withdrawing is the recurring cost.

Gate's spot fee at the base tier (VIP 0) is **0.10% maker and 0.10% taker**. If you pay fees with GT, the platform's own token, that drops to **0.09% / 0.09%** at the same tier. Perpetual futures at VIP 0 run **0.02% maker and 0.05% taker**. On a $60,000 position that's $30 as a taker versus $12 as a maker, which is why limit orders consistently beat market orders once size gets real.

Crypto deposits are free. Withdrawal fees are set per coin and network and Gate adjusts them roughly hourly based on congestion, so a TRX-based transfer is much cheaper than an ERC-20 one for the same USDT. Check the withdrawal screen before you send, not after.

Worth flagging: Gate adjusted its global spot and futures fee structure on **9 April 2026**, changing maker/taker rates and GT discounts across VIP tiers. Fee tables copied from older blog posts are wrong, including some that quote a 0.20% base spot rate.

## The parts that quietly eat your margin

**Cash-advance coding.** Plenty of banks treat a crypto card purchase as a cash advance: interest from day one, no grace period, sometimes an extra 3% to 5% on top. This is the single most under-reported cost in the whole process, and it's your bank's decision, not the exchange's. Call before you tap.

**Spread versus fee.** A "zero fee" swap still has a quoted price. Gate's flash convert is described as extremely low cost with a small spread, and that's a fair description. The honest way to measure a zero-fee route is to compare the quoted rate against a market reference, not to read the fee line.

**Region availability.** Gate restricts or prohibits some or all services in certain jurisdictions, including the US, Canada, Iran and Cuba per its own published disclaimer, and availability of specific payment routes varies by country, verification level and risk controls.

**P2P discipline.** The escrow only works if you stay inside it. Gate locks the seller's crypto during a trade and releases it once the seller confirms payment. Anyone suggesting you settle off-platform, or that you release before the money lands, is running a scam and no fee advantage justifies that risk.

## Which route fits which purchase

Buying a few hundred dollars and you want it now? Take the card, accept the 1% to 5%, and skip the ceremony. The absolute fee is small and you're paying for minutes, not days.

Buying regularly? P2P plus a limit order is the cheapest combination available, and it's the one strategy that holds up across venues. Gate charges buyers nothing on P2P, and a resting limit order at VIP 0 costs 0.10% instead of paying the taker rate on a market fill.

Buying five figures? Bank transfer, then work out where you'll land on the tier table before you start. At VIP 5 your spot maker fee is 0.09%; at VIP 10 it's **0%**. If you're planning $100,000 in volume over the next month, that difference is worth structuring your orders around rather than discovering a month later.

Already holding USDT somewhere? On-chain deposit costs nothing on Gate's side and skips the fiat rails entirely. You paid the network fee on the way out, which is usually cheaper than paying a processor 2.8% on the way in.
